What will Thursday's gas storage report show?

Each Thursday the US Energy Information Administration reports how much natural gas went into or out of underground storage the week before, and gas prices move when the number surprises traders. This model forecasts it from the weather before the report, using only what was known that morning.

How it works

Most of the week-to-week swing is weather: cold weeks burn gas for heat, hot weeks burn it in power plants for air conditioning.

  1. Measure the weather

    Daily temperatures from 17 cities become heating and cooling degree days, weighted toward the gas-heated North-East and Midwest in winter and Texas, the South and California in summer.

  2. Start from the standard guess

    The average change for the same week over the past five years, the number every storage report is compared with.

  3. Adjust for the weather

    A ridge regression learns how far colder or hotter than normal weather pushes storage away from that average, along with last week's surprise.

  4. Test it the hard way

    Every past week is predicted using only earlier weeks, as if it were that Thursday morning, then scored against what EIA actually reported.

What I found

Winter is where weather pays off

From November to March the model misses by 15 Bcf on average; the standard guess misses by 53. Cold snaps cause the big swings, and temperature data catches them.

Summer is harder to beat

From April to October the model misses by 12 Bcf against 17. Summer weeks swing less, and they depend on things the model doesn't see yet, like LNG exports and power demand.

Simple beat fancy

A plain linear model beat gradient-boosted trees on unseen weeks.

Big misses point to real events

The largest was Winter Storm Uri in February 2021: frozen wells cut supply, so storage fell further than the cold alone explains.

Terms

Bcf
Billion cubic feet of gas. The US uses roughly 90 Bcf a day; one Bcf heats about 15,000 homes for a year.
Injection and withdrawal
Gas added to storage (positive, mostly April to October) or taken out (negative, mostly winter).
5-year average
The average change for the same week over the past five years: the market's standard guess.
Degree days
How far each day's average temperature was below 65°F (heating) or above it (cooling), added up over the week.
Updated automatically every day after EIA's report; latest report in the data covers the week ending November 13, 2026. Data from EIA's weekly working gas in storage (Lower 48) and Open-Meteo temperatures. Built with Python, pandas and scikit-learn. Code on GitHub
